I get asked about this roughly once a month, usually by first-years at the club fair, so here is the long version.

## Why the combination is worth something

The useful thing is not that I know two subjects. It is that most interesting aerospace problems now have a software component that the aerospace curriculum doesn't teach and the CS curriculum doesn't motivate.

A concrete example from my lab: we had a CFD workflow where someone manually set up each case, ran it overnight, and copied results into a spreadsheet. Every aerospace student there understood the fluid dynamics perfectly. Nobody had thought of the workflow as a thing that could be *engineered* — versioned, tested, made reproducible. That gap is where the double major pays.

The reverse is also true. Plenty of strong programmers can build a data pipeline; far fewer can look at the output and say "that boundary layer separation point is wrong."

## What it costs

Mostly slack. There is no semester where I get to take an easy schedule, and the two degree requirements rarely overlap, so the credit count is close to additive rather than shared.

The specific thing to watch is that both majors have long prerequisite chains that only run once a year. Miss one and you don't lose a class, you lose a year. I have rearranged my entire schedule twice around a single course that is only offered in the fall.

## Advice I would actually give

**Map the prerequisite chains before your second semester.** Not the requirements list — the chains, with the semester each course is offered. Do it on paper. This is the single highest-leverage hour you will spend on your degree.

**Pick a real project early.** Coursework teaches you subjects. Projects teach you which subjects matter and how they connect. My rocketry team taught me more about state estimation than the class where I learned Kalman filters, because on the team the filter had to work on a vehicle that was actually going to fly.

**Don't optimize for the double major itself.** It is not a credential that impresses anyone on its own. What gets attention is being the person who can do the thing at the intersection. If you get the combination without getting that ability, you have paid the cost without collecting the benefit.

**Be honest about whether you want it.** If you like aerospace and merely tolerate programming, do aerospace with a CS minor and spend the recovered time on research. The double major is worth it if you genuinely want both, and expensive if you want one and are collecting the other.

## Where I am now

Going into junior year, the plan is more flight software and less coursework-for-its-own-sake. I want to spend next summer somewhere the code I write ends up on something that flies.

Ask me again in a year whether the schedule was worth it.
